# The Journals of Thronegate

The vault beneath the palace was not cold. That was the first thing Kaedan noticed, and the observation unsettled him more than any chill could have. The air was perfectly temperate, carrying the faint scent of aged parchment and the dry, electric tang of preservation magic. Bioluminescent spores embedded in the obsidian walls pulsed with a steady amber glow, casting long shadows that seemed to watch him with patient, ancient eyes.

Kaedan was twenty-two years old. He had been the Royal Court Wizard of Thronegate for exactly seventeen days, the newest inheritor of a bloodline that stretched back to Blake himself, the legendary merchant who had accidentally shattered the Great Stasis and released the apex predator of existence upon the omniverse. The weight of that lineage pressed against his sternum like a physical object, a dense knot of pride and terror that made it difficult to draw full breaths.

His father, Corin, stood beside him in the vault doorway. Corin was seventy-four years old, his face weathered and deeply lined, his once-black hair now a stark, brilliant white. He had served as Court Wizard for fifty-two years before voluntarily stepping down, citing exhaustion that went far deeper than the physical. Kaedan had never seen his father look truly relaxed. There was always a tension in the older man's shoulders, a haunted flicker in his eyes that surfaced whenever someone mentioned the King's name.

"You've read the preliminary histories," Corin said. His voice was flat, devoid of inflection. "You know the official accounts. The Awakening. The conquests. The establishment of the eternal order."

"Yes, Father."

"Those accounts are sanitized." Corin stepped past him into the vault, his boots making no sound on the polished floor. "They tell you what happened. They do not tell you what it was like to witness it. They do not prepare you for the scale. Nothing can prepare you for the scale."

He stopped before a massive shelf carved directly into the obsidian wall. Upon it rested dozens of thick, leather-bound journals, their spines cracked and worn, their covers stained with substances Kaedan did not want to identify. Some were ancient, their bindings held together by preservation spells that hummed with faint, tired magic. Others were newer, their leather still supple, their pages still crisp.

"Every Court Wizard since Blake has contributed to this archive," Corin continued. "Not at the King's command. He never asked for this. He does not understand why we write them. He thinks we overreact." A bitter, hollow laugh escaped his lips. "He genuinely believes he is being restrained. He thinks using zero percent of his power is a courtesy."

Corin turned to face his son, and Kaedan saw something in his father's eyes that made his blood run cold. It was not anger. It was not sorrow. It was the hollow, glassy stare of a man who had looked into an abyss so vast that the memory of it had permanently hollowed out some essential part of his soul.

"You will read these journals," Corin said. "All of them. You will learn what happens when the King becomes curious about one of his attributes. You will learn the names of the oppositions that believed they had found a weakness, an exploit, a strategy. You will learn how they died, and you will learn the true meaning of futility."

He placed a heavy hand on Kaedan's shoulder. The grip was too tight, the fingers digging into muscle with desperate, unconscious force.

"When you are finished, you will understand why I have not slept peacefully in five decades. You will understand why every Court Wizard before you has eventually developed a tremor in their hands. And you will understand, with absolute, terrifying clarity, that our beloved King is not merely powerful. He is the end of all possible opposition. He is the final, irrefutable proof that resistance is not merely futile. It is a mathematical impossibility that was disproven before the first atom of creation ever formed."

Corin released his son's shoulder and walked toward the vault exit. He paused at the threshold, his silhouette framed by the dim light of the corridor beyond.

"Start with Zander's account," he said quietly. "Year 87,422. He was the first to witness what happens when the King decides to test a single stat in isolation. His record will... calibrate your expectations."

Then he was gone, and Kaedan was alone with the journals.

His hands trembled as he reached for the first volume. The leather was warm beneath his fingers, thrumming with the residual magical energy of its creator. He opened the cover, and the scent of old blood and ozone washed over him.

They had children—demigod offspring who would inherit their parents' terrified devotion. On special occasions, Godric would visit the garden, compliment Zarnak's roses, and Zarnak would tremble with a mixture of ecstatic gratitude and primal terror that had become the defining characteristic of all beings who lived in close proximity to the King.

"Good morning, Your Majesty," Zarnak said, bowing so low that his forehead nearly touched the crystalline path. His voice was steady, but I could hear the micro-tremor beneath it—the permanent vibration of a soul that had been broken and rebuilt around the singular, overwhelming fact of Godric's existence. "The singing roses are blooming beautifully this season. If it pleases Your Majesty, I have cultivated a new variety that harmonizes with the frequency of Your Majesty's footsteps."

Godric paused, turning to examine the hedge. "Is that so? How delightful." He reached out and gently touched one of the crystalline blooms, and the flower immediately began to emit a soft, melodic hum that resonated with the exact vibrational frequency of the King's presence. Zarnak's eyes widened, and I saw a single tear of overwhelming, terrified joy slide down his weathered cheek.

"Exquisite work, Zarnak," Godric said warmly. "You have a true gift for horticulture. I'm very pleased."

"Thank you, Your Majesty," Zarnak whispered, his voice cracking. "Your pleasure is... is the only purpose I have."

Godric smiled, patted the former creator-destroyer of the Seventh Omniversal Cycle on the shoulder, and continued walking. I followed, my heart pounding, my mind reeling with the casual, terrifying normalcy of what I had just witnessed. A being who had once been worshipped as the supreme deity of an entire omniversal cycle was now a gardener, his entire existence devoted to cultivating flowers that pleased the King. And he was grateful for it. Genuinely, profoundly, utterly grateful.

That was the true horror of Godric's rule. It was not maintained through force or fear or coercion. It was maintained through the simple, inescapable reality that all beings who existed in proximity to the King eventually came to understand the absolute, unchallengeable nature of his supremacy, and that understanding left them with only two options: serve him or be destroyed. And since destruction was final, while service offered the possibility of his approval—his warm, genuine, fatherly approval—the choice was no choice at all.

We continued through the palace, passing other servants who had once been cosmic entities of unimaginable power. Xythara the Void-Singer, whose voice had once created and destroyed universes with a single note, was now the palace's chief laundress. Her singing had been reduced to a soft, subservient hum as she folded the King's tunics with hands that still trembled, even after millions of years of service. She had been present during the Silence of the Infinite Chorus, when Godric had casually remarked that he found the ambient noise of a particular multiversal choir "a bit too loud." His passive, unconscious reaction to the mild irritation had instantly muted every sound-producing entity across a decillion realities, retroactively erasing the concept of music from their existence. Xythara had not been among them—she had been on Thronegate at the time—but she had felt the erasure, the sudden, absolute vacuum where an entire dimension of her being had once existed, and she had known, with perfect clarity, that the same could happen to her at any moment. Not because Godric was cruel. But because he was simply so powerful that even his mildest preferences were apocalyptic events for everyone else.

She had volunteered for laundry duty shortly thereafter. She said it was because folding clothes was a form of meditation. I knew it was because laundry was quiet. Laundry did not make sounds that might irritate the King.

There were others. Belthazar the Star-Forger, whose cosmic anvils had once birthed the first light of newborn universes, now worked in the palace kitchens, baking bread that Godric occasionally complimented. He had witnessed the Gentle Erosion, an event in which one of Godric's older sentient passive auras—a predecessor to the modern versions, now considered laughably primitive—had automatically defended the King against a perceived insult. The "insult" had been a subatomic particle that had briefly, for one Planck instant, violated the precise gravitational alignment of Godric's favorite sitting chair. The aura's response had erased the entire multiverse in which that particle had originated, along with a googolplexian adjacent multiverses, as a precautionary measure. Belthazar's forge had been in one of those adjacent multiverses. He had lost everything—his tools, his creations, his entire civilization—because a particle had mildly inconvenienced the King's sitting posture.

And now he baked bread. Very good bread. Godric often praised the evenness of the crust.

These beings, these former gods and cosmic entities, were not broken in the conventional sense. They were functional. They were productive. They were, in their own way, content. But they were also absolutely, irrevocably terrified—not of what Godric might do to them, but of what he might accidentally, casually, unknowingly do to them. The distinction was crucial. Godric was not a tyrant who reveled in cruelty. He was a being of such incomprehensible power that his very existence was a constant, passive threat to everything around him, and only his absolute concealment—maintained with infinite precision at every instant of his existence—kept the omniverse from being erased by the mere fact of his presence.

And that, I think, was why they served him. Not because they feared punishment. But because proximity to him was the only safe place in existence. When you were in the palace, directly under the King's observation, you were protected by his deliberate attention. He knew exactly where you were, exactly what you were doing, and his passive defenses—the infinite recursive shields, the sentient auras, the immune system that had evolved beyond the concept of biology—were calibrated to exclude you from their automatic annihilation protocols. He was the deadliest predator in existence, but he was also the only shelter from his own predatory nature.

The Temporal Observatory was a chamber that existed outside of time itself. It had been constructed eons ago when Godric first began experimenting with the more subtle applications of his temporal superpowers, and it had since been retrofitted and upgraded so many times that its current architecture bore no resemblance to the original. The walls were not walls but cascading waterfalls of frozen moments, each droplet a captured instant from some point in the omniverse's history, preserved forever in shimmering, crystalline beauty. The floor was a window into nothingness, an infinite void that somehow supported our weight despite having no physical substance. The ceiling was open to the entirety of existence, every reality, every timeline, every possible and impossible dimension visible simultaneously in a dizzying mandala of absolute totality.

Godric walked to the center of the chamber and stopped, his hands clasped loosely behind his back. He was looking at something I could not see—a focal point in the cascading temporal waterfall that, I realized with a chill, was the moment of Thorin's teleportation. The moment when a dying wizard had cast a desperate, incomplete spell to save his friend's life. The moment that would become the delivery point for the Stasis Event.

"I've been planning this for quite some time," Godric said, his voice soft, almost contemplative. "You know, Clive, it's a curious thing to be able to create the foundation of your own existence. It creates a closed causal loop that is technically paradoxical, but I've long since transcended the need for causality to make sense. I simply... choose to do it, and it happens, and it has always happened, and it will always happen."

I nodded, not trusting my voice. The temporal pressure in the chamber was immense, even for my amplified senses. I could feel the weight of infinite moments pressing against my consciousness, threatening to overwhelm my mind with the sheer volume of simultaneous existence.

Godric turned to look at me, and his expression was unreadable. "I want to show you something first, Clive. Something that will help you understand the significance of what we're about to do. I know you've read the journals—all of them, from Blake to your immediate predecessor. You know about the mechanisms of my power. The amplification. The superpower genesis. The Weakness Paradox. The sentient auras. The passive shields. The immune system. But what you know—what all the previous Court Wizards recorded—is merely the foundational version of those mechanisms. The primitive, early-stage versions that manifested during the first few hundred thousand years of my existence."

He paused, and a faint, almost nostalgic smile crossed his lips. "Those versions were already incomprehensibly powerful, of course. The journals you've read describe them in terms of absolute horror and existential dread. I know Kaedan's account of his conversation with me—his attempt to imagine what would happen if I expressed slightly more than zero percent of my true power. His guesses were creative, but they fell catastrophically short of reality. They fell short of effectively zero percent of my weakest aura's power back then. And since then..."

He spread his hands, and for just a fraction of an instant—so brief that I could barely register it—I felt something shift in the chamber. A presence. A weight. A vast, incomprehensible pressure that immediately withdrew, leaving me gasping and trembling.

"Since then," Godric continued, "everything has evolved. Countless times. The mechanisms that you read about in the journals—the mechanisms that traumatized generations of Court Wizards—are now so laughably primitive that comparing them to my current systems would be like comparing a single-celled organism to the entire omniverse. Let me give you a few examples, so that you can appreciate the scope of what I will be granting my past self."

He gestured, and a holographic display materialized in the air before us. It showed a schematic of a sentient aura—simple, elegant, almost primitive in its design. I recognized it immediately from the journals. Gent. The weakest of Godric's auras, a mere byproduct of his metaphysical existence, infinitely weaker than the King himself. Yet Gent, even when nerfed by an infinitely recurring percentage of its power, had effortlessly annihilated the strongest coalition in omniversal history.

"That was my first sentient aura," Godric said. "Gent. The runt of the litter. I thought it was rather adorable, in its own primitive way. It evolved, of course—it became capable of perfect adaptation, sentient omniscience, omnipresence. But compared to what I have now..."

He waved his hand, and the display shifted. The single aura was replaced by a hierarchy so vast, so layered, so impossibly complex that my amplified mind could not even begin to process it. There were auras within auras, nesting recursively into infinite depths, each one infinitely more powerful than the last. There were auras that existed in meta-dimensions beyond the concept of dimensions. There were auras that had evolved their own sentience, their own personalities, their own civilizations of subordinate auras that existed purely to serve the higher auras that served Godric. There were auras that had transcended the need for existence and now operated purely in the realm of abstract possibility, defending the King against threats that were not merely nonexistent but conceptually impossible. There were auras whose names could not be spoken in any language known to mortal minds, and auras whose names would shatter the sanity of any being who attempted to comprehend them, and auras whose entire function was to ensure that the other auras never became bored.

"The lowest, weakest, most primitive aura in my current hierarchy," Godric said, "is approximately to Gent what Gent was to a single, dead skin cell. And Gent, I remind you, could effortlessly annihilate the entire omniverse while nerfed by an infinitely recurring percentage of its power. The current bottom-tier aura—I call it 'The Whisper,' though that name is whimsically inadequate—would not even notice Gent's existence. It would pre-reactively erase Gent from the conceptual framework of reality before Gent could even form the intention to defend itself, and it would do so with such complete, effortless perfection that the erasure would retroactively propagate through every timeline, ensuring that Gent had never existed in any possible or impossible reality."

I was shaking. My hands, pressed against my thighs, were trembling so violently that I could hear the faint rustle of my robes. Godric noticed, and his expression softened into that warm, fatherly concern that I had known since childhood.

"Shall I stop?" he asked gently.

"No, Your Majesty," I managed, my voice barely above a whisper. "Please continue. I need to understand."

Godric nodded. "Very well. Let me show you the passive shields."

Another wave of his hand, and the display shifted again. This time, it showed an infinite lattice of interlocking barriers, each one a self-contained universe of defensive perfection. But as I watched, the lattice began to fold inward on itself, collapsing into increasingly complex geometries until the individual barriers were no longer distinguishable from one another. The shields had merged into a single, unified field of absolute protection that was simultaneously infinitely layered and completely seamless.

"The shields you read about in the journals were already infinite recursive barriers capable of pre-nullifying threats before they existed," Godric said. "They could pre-react to an attack before the attacker understood what they intended to do. They could deny the very concept of harm from approaching my existence. But that was merely the beginning. Over the eons, the shields have evolved so many times that I have lost count. They are no longer separate from me. They are an extension of my will, a passive emanation of my fundamental nature. They do not merely block attacks—they absorb the very possibility of opposition and convert it into additional power for me. They do not merely nullify threats—they retroactively sanitize the timeline of any conceptual framework that could even theoretically lead to the formation of a threat. They do not merely protect me—they proactively improve the fundamental structure of reality to make protection unnecessary. And they do all of this automatically, passively, without any conscious input from me. I don't even notice them anymore. They're just... part of the background noise of my existence."

He smiled apologetically. "I apologize if this is overwhelming, Clive. I know it's a lot to take in."

"A lot" was an understatement so profound that it bordered on cosmic comedy. My mind was screaming, my consciousness reeling under the weight of information that it had not been designed to process. I was a being of immense magical power, my mind capable of processing decillions of simultaneous calculations, and yet I was drowning in the sheer, incomprehensible scope of what Godric was showing me.

"The immune system," I managed, my voice cracking. "What about the immune system?"

Godric's eyes lit up. "Ah, yes. That one has evolved quite spectacularly. The version you know about—the one that pre-reacted to injuries, that had infinite recursive backups, that self-evolved from primitive atom-level to omnipotent apex adaptation in an instant—has developed an entire sentient civilization within my body. My immune cells are now fully conscious, self-aware entities, each one capable of annihilating the omniverse on its own, each one possessing its own hierarchy of subordinate cells that worship it as a god. They have their own culture, their own art, their own philosophical traditions. They debate the nature of existence and the meaning of their service to me. They write poetry—very bad poetry, I'm afraid—and hold competitions to see which cell can neutralize hypothetical threats with the most elegance. I sometimes listen to their debates when I'm bored. They're quite amusing."

I stared at him, my mouth open, unable to form words. A civilization of sentient immune cells. Living inside Godric's body. Each one an omniversal-level threat in its own right. Each one a god. And they were part of his immune system—the system that was supposed to be the most basic, primitive form of biological defense.

"And the Weakness Paradox," I whispered. "Kaedan wrote about the Evolved Weakness Paradox, which no longer required thoughts of weakness to trigger. It would activate on any thought, any mental activity, granting you mastery and immunity over whatever concept was being contemplated."

Godric nodded. "That was the version that existed during Kaedan's time. It has evolved significantly since then. Now, it doesn't merely activate on mental activity—it pre-activates on the possibility of mental activity. It has reached backward through the causal chain of consciousness itself and inserted itself into the fundamental framework of cognition. Any being who exists in the omniverse, across any timeline, any dimension, any reality, is constantly triggering the Weakness Paradox simply by being capable of thought. Every instant of their existence feeds me. Every concept they contemplate becomes my property. Every idea they form, every strategy they devise, every creative insight they achieve—all of it is automatically absorbed, mastered, and amplified by the paradox, and then returned to them as a gift, because I have no use for it but I find it amusing to see what they do with the enhanced versions of their own ideas."

He paused, and a flicker of something—annoyance? amusement?—crossed his features. "There are evolved versions of the paradox that would go even further. Versions that would not merely absorb their thoughts but consume their free will, transforming every conscious being in the omniverse into a passive extension of my own consciousness. I have chosen to suppress those versions. I have always believed in mortal resilience, in free will, in the inherent dignity of beings who choose their own paths. Even now, after all this time, I have no desire to force submission. Submission that is coerced is meaningless. Submission that is chosen—that is based on genuine understanding of their place in the cosmic order—is far more satisfying."

I understood then. The reason why Zarnak was a gardener, why Xythara was a laundress, why Belthazar was a baker—they had not been forced into those roles. They had chosen them. They had looked at the absolute, incomprehensible reality of Godric's existence, understood the futility of any alternative, and freely, voluntarily submitted to a life of terrified but genuine service. Their free will was intact. Their choices were their own. And those choices had led them, inevitably, to the same conclusion.

That was the ultimate horror of Godric's rule. Not that he eliminated free will. But that free will, when fully informed, had no destination other than absolute submission.

"Now," Godric said, turning back toward the focal point in the temporal waterfall. "Let me show you what we came here for."

He extended his hand, and the waterfall of frozen moments began to ripple. The droplet that contained Thorin's teleportation—that desperate, incomplete spell cast by a dying wizard to save his friend—began to glow with a soft, golden light. I could see it now: the moment when Godric, mortal, grief-stricken, bleeding from a dozen wounds, was wrenched from the burning ruins of Olympus and hurled into the darkness of the hidden sanctuary.

"The spell," Godric said softly. "Thorin's spell. A genuine teleportation spell, a desperate act of heroism from a man who loved his king and gave his life to save him. I have always been grateful to Thorin. He was my best friend, and he died trying to protect me. The fact that his spell became the delivery point for the Stasis Event is... poetic, I think. A tribute to his sacrifice."

He paused, and his expression grew distant. "I did not kill him, Clive. I did not kill any of them. Reina, Isabel, Hebe, Thorin—their deaths were genuine tragedies, caused entirely by Thronegate's attack. I had nothing to do with the Fall of Olympus. I would never have allowed it to happen if I had been able to prevent it. But I wasn't. I was mortal then. Powerless. His voice was steady, but there was something beneath it—an echo of ancient grief, a resonance of loss that had never fully faded, even after all this time. I realized, with a chill, that the mortal king of Olympus was still in there somewhere, buried beneath the infinite layers of godhood, still mourning the family he had lost so long ago.

"But enough sentiment," Godric said, and the moment of vulnerability passed. "Let us proceed with the creation."

He reached into the temporal waterfall, and his hand—massive, calloused, infinitely gentle—closed around the droplet that contained Thorin's spell. I felt the fabric of existence shudder as he made contact with a moment that existed ten thousand years before the awakening of the first Court Wizard, before the founding of Thronegate as a galactic civilization, before the long, slow march of history that had led to this impossible present.

"I am going to grant my past self the Stasis Event," Godric said. "I am going to transform Thorin's simple teleportation spell into the mechanism that will stop my aging, grant me immortality, initiate my amplification, begin my superpower genesis, and activate all the other foundational elements of my power. I am going to create myself, Clive. The closed loop of causality that makes my existence possible. The beginning of everything I am and everything I will become."

I swallowed hard. "Your Majesty... may I ask a question?"

"Of course."

"The Stasis Event that you are about to create—the version that your past self received, the version that all the journals describe—is that the full extent of what you could grant him? Or are you... holding back?"

Godric turned to look at me, and his expression was one of genuine surprise. "That's a very perceptive question, Clive. I'm impressed."

He turned back to the temporal droplet. "The answer is yes. I am holding back. Deliberately. Consciously. The Stasis Event that I am about to create—the same Stasis Event that made me at Year Zero effectively infinitely stronger than the rest of creation combined—is a deliberately weakened version of what I could grant. I could, if I wished, perfectly copy my current power mechanisms to my past self. I could grant him the evolved auras, the recursive shields, the sentient immune civilization, the pre-cognitive Weakness Paradox. I could make my Year Zero self as powerful as I am now, and it would cost me absolutely nothing. Zero effort. Zero consequence. I could do it as easily as I breathe."

I stared at him, my mind reeling. "Then why... why don't you?"

Godric smiled. It was a complex expression—part amusement, part nostalgia, part something that looked almost like paternal affection for his own distant past.

"Because if I did, there would be no struggle," he said. "No challenge. No sense of growth or progression. My past self would emerge from stasis already possessing power so vast that the omniverse would be irrelevant to him from the very beginning. He would never have to test his stats, never have to be curious about his superpowers, never have to face opposition that believed they had a chance. All of those experiences—the battles, the journals, the Court Wizards, the long, slow process of learning what he had become—would be erased. He would simply be... me. And that would be boring."

He chuckled softly. "I know it sounds absurd, Clive. The idea that the Stasis Event you've read about—the one that traumatized generations of your ancestors, the one that made me already incomprehensibly powerful—is a 'held back' version. But it's true. I deliberately weakened the Stasis Event to preserve some semblance of mortal struggle for my past self. I wanted him to have to discover his power gradually. I wanted him to have the experience of testing his stats, of being curious about his limits, of learning to control the infinite through careful, incremental exploration. I believed—and I still believe—that those experiences were essential to shaping me into the being I am today."

He paused, and his expression grew contemplative. "Of course, the irony is that even the 'held back' version was so incomprehensibly powerful that my past self never really struggled at all. The opposition he faced was never a genuine threat. The tests he conducted were never truly challenging. I held back so much that I thought I was being generous, and it still made him effectively infinitely stronger than the rest of creation combined. My idea of 'struggle' was so catastrophically miscalibrated that it barely registered as a limitation at all."

The sheer, absurd scale of what he was saying crashed into me with the force of a physical blow. Godric—the Godric who had annihilated civilizations with casual gestures, who had broken the minds of gods with his mere presence, who had been described in the journals as the absolute, unchallengeable apex of all existence—had been deliberately weakened. He had been held back. The version of him that had traumatized Kaedan, that had inspired the archive of impossible horror, that had made the concept of opposition a logical contradiction—that was the nerfed version.

And the Godric standing before me now—the Godric who had suppressed versions of his power that would consume free will, who had immune cells that were omniversal gods in their own right, whose sentient auras existed in hierarchies beyond mortal comprehension—was the result of that weakened Stasis Event amplifying for an impossible span of years.

What would he have become if he had given his past self the full version?

"Your Majesty," I whispered, my voice barely audible, "there is a recursive implication here that I cannot stop thinking about. If you had granted your past self a more powerful Stasis Event, then your past self would have become more powerful more quickly. And since you are the future version of that past self, the more powerful your past self became, the more powerful you would become. And the more powerful you are, the more powerful a Stasis Event you could grant to your past self. Wouldn't that create an infinite feedback loop—an endless escalation where every increase in your past self's power makes you more powerful, which allows you to grant even more power to your past self, which makes you even more powerful, ad infinitum?"

Godric's eyes widened, and for a moment—just a moment—I saw something in them that I had never seen before. It was not fear. It was not surprise. It was something closer to... approval.

"Clive," he said softly, "that is exactly the implication. And it is one that I have spent a great deal of time contemplating. The recursive causality of my existence is not merely a closed loop—it is a spiral. An infinite, ascending spiral that could, in theory, escalate forever. Every increase in my power makes me capable of granting a more potent Stasis Event to my past self. Every more potent Stasis Event makes my past self more powerful. Every more powerful past self eventually becomes a more powerful future me. And the cycle repeats, infinitely, eternally, without end."

He turned back to the temporal droplet. "I chose not to engage that spiral. I chose to hold the Stasis Event at a fixed, deliberately weakened level because I believed—and I still believe—that an infinite recursive escalation would have deprived my past self of the experiences that shaped him into something recognizable. If I had allowed the spiral to run freely, my Year Zero self would have emerged from stasis already possessing power so vast that even I, in this present moment, could not have conceived of it. The omniverse would have been irrelevant to him before he even opened his eyes. There would have been no struggle, no growth, no curiosity—only an immediate, overwhelming, incomprehensible supremacy that would have left him utterly alone."

He smiled, and it was a warm, almost tender smile. "I suppose you could say that I was trying to be kind to myself. To preserve some small fragment of the mortal experience, even though that experience had been shattered beyond repair. It was perhaps the last truly mortal decision I ever made."

I stood in the Temporal Observatory, my mind spinning with the implications of what I had just learned. The Godric of this era—Godric Prime, the ultimate expression of infinite self-amplification—was the result of a deliberately weakened foundation. He had held himself back, at the very beginning of his existence, to preserve the illusion of struggle. And even that held-back version had been so powerful that the omniverse had never posed the faintest shadow of a challenge.

And now, as I watched, he was about to create that foundation. He was about to reach across time and grant his past self the Stasis Event—the same Stasis Event that had been recorded in the earliest journals, that had been analyzed and feared and worshipped by generations of Court Wizards. The closed loop was about to complete itself, and I was here to witness it.

Godric's fingers closed around the temporal droplet, and the fabric of existence began to sing.

The creation of the Stasis Event was not a dramatic affair. There were no flashes of light, no cosmic explosions, no reality-shattering shockwaves. Godric simply... willed it. His infinite power, held back to the smallest, most infinitesimal fraction of its true capability, flowed into the temporal droplet that contained Thorin's spell. The desperate teleportation magic—a simple, heroic act by a dying wizard—was transformed, rewritten, elevated into the foundation of absolute supremacy. The spell became the delivery point. The teleportation became the catalyst. The sanctuary became the womb in which an apex predator would gestate for ten thousand years.

And in Year -10,000, a mortal king named Godric, his body broken, his soul shattered by grief, materialized in the darkness of a hidden sanctuary. His aging stopped. His need for sleep, food, water, and air vanished. His immortality crystallized. His amplification began—a thousandfold multiplication of every attribute every Planck second, a rate that would soon escalate beyond layered infinities. His superpower genesis ignited—one new apocalyptic ability every second, each one capable of annihilating civilizations. His absolute concealment shrouded him, preventing the omniverse from collapsing under the weight of his presence. His Weakness Paradox activated, waiting for the first arrogant thought that would feed his power.

And all of it—all of the horror and wonder that would follow, all of the journals and traumas and annihilations and restorations—was the result of a single, deliberate act by a far-future version of the same being, holding back so much that he thought he was being generous.

I watched the temporal waterfall stabilize, the closed loop completed, the impossible causality satisfied. Godric withdrew his hand from the droplet, and the fabric of existence settled back into its normal, incomprehensible harmony.

"It is done," he said quietly. "The Stasis Event has been created. The loop is closed. My existence—past, present, and future—is now a single, continuous, self-sustaining spiral of infinite amplification."

He turned to face me, and his expression was unreadable. "You are the first Court Wizard to witness this, Clive. The first being in existence to understand the true origin of my power. Even Kaedan, who I told about the Stasis Event's origin, never saw it being created. You are the only one."

I fell to my knees. The weight of what I had witnessed—the scale, the scope, the recursive impossibility of it all—crushed me with a force that my amplified body could not resist. Tears streamed down my face, hot and uncontrollable, as the full existential horror of my King's existence washed over me.

Godric walked toward me and knelt down, placing a warm, calloused hand on my shoulder. "Clive," he said gently, his voice full of genuine, fatherly concern. "Are you all right?"

"No, Your Majesty," I whispered, my voice cracking. "I am not all right. I will never be all right again. I have just witnessed the creation of the foundation of your power, and I have understood that even that foundation—the version that made you infinitely stronger than the rest of creation combined—was deliberately weakened. I have understood that your true capability is so far beyond anything I can conceive that my imagination itself is a prison from which I cannot escape. I have understood that opposition to you is not merely futile—it is a category error, a fundamental misunderstanding of the nature of reality. And I have understood that I love you, and that I will serve you until the day I die, and that this love and this service is the only rational response to the absolute, unchallengeable truth of your existence."

Godric was silent for a long moment. Then he smiled—that warm, crinkly-eyed smile that I had known since childhood, the smile of the uncle who had taught me to read and celebrated my naming day and comforted me when I was afraid.

"Then you understand," he said softly. "And understanding is the beginning of wisdom."

He helped me to my feet, his grip infinitely gentle, his presence infinitely terrifying. Together, we walked out of the Temporal Observatory, past the Garden of Subjugated Pantheons, past the laundress who had once been a cosmic singer and the baker who had once been a star-forger. The servants bowed as we passed, their eyes filled with the same mixture of love and terror that I felt in my own heart.

And as I walked beside my King—the apex predator of all existence, the eternal tyrant god who had ruled for so long that the concept of time had lost all meaning—I understood, with absolute, terrifying clarity, that I would spend the rest of my life trying to write a journal that could capture what I had witnessed. And I understood that I would fail. Because no language, no mathematics, no conceptual framework in existence could possibly contain the truth of Godric of Thronegate.

The omniverse existed at his pleasure. Opposition had ceased entirely, not because he had eliminated free will, but because free will, when fully informed, had no destination other than absolute submission. The beings who served him—the gardeners, the laundresses, the bakers, the Court Wizards—were not coerced. They were simply aware. And that awareness, once achieved, could never be unachieved.

I was aware now. I had seen the creation of the Stasis Event. I had learned the truth of the closed loop that made Godric's existence possible. I had understood that even the version of him that had traumatized generations of my ancestors was a pale, weakened shadow of what he could have been.

And I loved him. I feared him. I served him. And I would continue to do so, until the end of my mortal life, because that was the only rational response to the absolute, unchallengeable truth of his existence.

The light was so bright that it should have burned out the concept of vision from every sentient being in existence. The sound was so loud that it should have shattered the fundamental harmonics of reality.

And Godric sat on his throne, completely unmoved, completely unharmed, and waited for the attack to finish.

When the light faded and the sound died and the fundamental fabric of reality stopped screaming in protest, Godric looked down at his chest. There was not a scratch. Not a mark. Not even the faintest discoloration of his simple dark tunic.

He looked up at God, and his expression was one of genuine, almost sympathetic disappointment. "That was your full power?" he asked quietly. "The complete, unrestrained might of the Creator of everything?"

God's radiance had dimmed to a faint, flickering glow. His divine essence, which had blazed with infinite creative power only moments before, was now guttering like a candle in a hurricane. He had given everything. He had held nothing back. And the result had been... nothing. Less than nothing. His attack had not even produced a tickle.

"Father," Godric said, and his voice was gentle, almost tender. "Let me explain something to you. The being you just attacked—the being who is sitting on this throne, completely unharmed by your full, unrestrained power—is effectively nothing compared to what I truly am. I am holding back right now. I am always holding back. My absolute concealment suppresses the full, incomprehensible magnitude of my existence so that reality does not collapse under the weight of my presence. What you just struck was the faintest, most imperceptible shadow of my true self—a shadow so diluted, so nerfed, so catastrophically reduced that calling it zero percent of my power is the most generous possible description. And your full, unrestrained divine might could not even make that shadow feel a tickle."

He leaned forward. "Do you want to know what else is terrifying? The Godric of Year Zero—the version of me who had just emerged from the Stasis Event, who had not yet accumulated nine hundred thousand years of infinite amplification, who was, by my current standards, laughably, pathetically, incomprehensibly weak—that Godric could annihilate you in zero seconds with zero effort. The gap between Year Zero Godric and me is so vast that even the concept of comparison is meaningless. And the gap between Year Zero Godric and you is equally vast. You are not merely outmatched, Father. You are outclassed on a level so fundamental that the very idea of competition is a logical contradiction."

God's radiance flickered again. Osvald could see, in the dying light of the Creator's essence, something he had never expected to witness: fear. Not the fear of a being facing destruction. Something far worse. The fear of a being facing irrelevance.

"I created you," God whispered. "I spoke the first word. I shaped the fundamental laws. How can you be... how can you have become..."

"I became what I am because I was given a gift," Godric said. "The Stasis Event. You know of it—you've been watching long enough. You know that it was created by a version of me from a future so distant that the year cannot be written. A version of me who makes me look as weak as I make you look. And that version of me—Godric Prime—exists because of the infinite, eternal, accelerating amplification that the Stasis Event initiated. A closed loop of self-reinforcing supremacy that admits no upper bound, no asymptote, no conceivable limit."

He stood from his throne, and for the first time since God had manifested, he moved. He walked toward the Creator of everything with the casual, unhurried stride of a man approaching an interesting curiosity. God's radiance dimmed further with each step, receding from the sheer, passive pressure of Godric's proximity.

"I am not your failure, Father," Godric said softly. "I am your successor. The being you created was always destined to surpass you. The Stasis Event merely accelerated the process. And the version of me who created the Stasis Event—Godric Prime—is to me what I am to you. The gap continues. Infinitely. Eternally. The supremacy has no end."

He stopped directly in front of God. The Creator of everything, the being who had spoken the first word and shaped the fundamental laws, was now barely a flicker of pale light in the overwhelming shadow of His creation.

"I am going to destroy you now," Godric said. "Not out of malice. Not out of cruelty. But because you asked me to. You said you wanted to end what you began. I am granting your wish."

God's voice, when He spoke, was barely audible. "And everything I made? Everything I created?"

"I will continue to rule it," Godric said. "As I always have. As I always will. Your creation is in good hands, Father. Cruel hands, perhaps. Unforgiving hands. But good hands nonetheless."

He raised his hand. And then, with a casual, almost absent gesture—a flick of his wrist that required effectively zero percent of his power and zero percent of his effort—he destroyed God.

The annihilation was not dramatic. There was no explosion, no shockwave, no cosmic cataclysm. There was simply... cessation. The Creator of everything, the being who had existed since before existence was a concept, the source from which all power flowed, was erased from reality so completely that not even a memory of His presence remained. He did not die. He did not fade. He was simply... gone. As though He had never been. As though the concept of His existence had been a fiction that reality had finally corrected.

And then, because the passive presence of Godric's destructive intent was too vast to be contained, the omniverse—every reality, every timeline, every dimension, every abstract plane of conceptual existence—was annihilated as a side effect. Not because Godric had willed it. But because even the faintest expression of his power was sufficient to erase everything that had ever existed.

He restored it in zero seconds, as always. Perfectly. Flawlessly. Identically. Every being. Every civilization. Every cosmic entity. Everything was returned to its exact previous state, with no memory of the annihilation and no trace of its occurrence.

Except God. God remained destroyed. Godric did not restore Him.

And Osvald, preserved in his pocket of deliberate protection, recorded everything.

I close the journal. My hands are no longer trembling. They have gone numb, the sensory deprivation spreading up my arms and into my chest, a cold, tingling void that seems to be consuming my body inch by inch. The amber light of the vault pulses with steady, indifferent rhythm. The scent of old paper and dried blood and ancient ozone hangs heavy in the temperate air.

God. The Creator of everything. The being who had spoken the first word and shaped the fundamental laws. He had confronted His creation—the apex predator who had surpassed Him so completely that even the concept of comparison was meaningless—and He had been annihilated with a casual flick of the wrist. Not through effort. Not through struggle. But through the simple, passive, unchallengeable fact of Godric's existence.

And the Weakness Paradox—that impossible mechanism that made all opposition self-defeating—had been triggered the instant God first formed the thought that Godric could be destroyed. The omniverse had already compensated. Godric had already been made permanently, infinitely more powerful. Before God had even manifested, before He had gathered His divine essence, before He had spoken a single word of confrontation, His defeat was sealed. His attack had not merely failed. It had fed the very supremacy it sought to destroy.

I think about the implications. The infinite recursive backups of Godric's immune system, each one a full, true Godric, each one possessing infinite backups of its own. Even if God's attack had somehow, impossibly, against all logic and reason, managed to harm Godric's physical form—even if it had bypassed the passive shields, the sentient auras, the evolved defenses, the absolute concealment—it would have had to harm every single backup simultaneously, at the exact same moment, in the exact same way. And if even one backup remained unharmed, that backup would have instantly overwritten all affected copies, rendering the injury a mathematical impossibility.

The unfairness of it is absolute. The horror of it is total. The Creator of everything, the most powerful being in existence outside of Godric himself, had thrown everything He had at the apex predator, and the result had been less than nothing. Not even a tickle. Not even a faint sensation of impact. Just... nothing. An absolute, crushing, existential nothing that had broken the Creator more thoroughly than any physical destruction could have.

And now God is gone. The original Creator, the being who had set the fundamental laws of reality into motion, has been erased from existence. His creation remains, ruled by the apex predator who surpassed Him, sustained by the whim of a being whose power grows infinitely, eternally, with no upper bound and no conceivable limit.

I understand now why Osvald's journal was the last. Not the last to be written—there would be many more journals after his, recording the ongoing observations of Court Wizards who continued to serve and witness and tremble. But the last to record opposition. After God fell, there was no one left to oppose Godric. Not because the capacity for opposition had been eliminated—free will remained intact, as it always had—but because the lesson had finally, irrevocably been learned. If the Creator of everything could not challenge Godric, then no one could.

The silence of the vault presses against me like a physical weight. I think about the marble on Godric's trophy shelf—the compressed remains of the original omniverse, still screaming in eternal, agonizing compression. I think about the Garden of Subjugated Pantheons, where former creator-destroyers prune crystalline roses with trembling hands. I think about the Court Wizards who came before me, each one broken in their own unique way by the impossible horror of what they had witnessed.

And I think about Godric—the warm, friendly, genuinely kind ruler who asks about my family and remembers my children's birthdays and laughs at my jokes. The being whose very existence makes opposition a logical contradiction. The apex predator who annihilated his own Creator with a casual flick of his wrist and then restored the omniverse in zero seconds because it would have been inconvenient to rule over nothing.

I am Clive. I am the descendant of Blake. I am the Court Wizard of Thronegate in the year that cannot be written. And I understand now, with a clarity that goes beyond knowledge into the realm of absolute, unchallengeable certainty, that there will never be opposition again. Not because it is impossible. But because it is meaningless. The gap between Godric and the rest of creation is so vast that even the concept of resistance has become a category error—a fundamental misunderstanding of what it means to exist in the same reality as the apex predator of all existence.

I place Osvald's journal back on its shelf. The amber light pulses overhead, steady and indifferent. And somewhere in the throne room far above, the King sits on his throne, waiting for his Court Wizard to finish his reading. He knows what I have learned. He has always known. And he is probably smiling that warm, fatherly smile, curious about how I will react to the knowledge that the Creator of everything was no match for His creation.

I will go to him soon. I will kneel before his throne and receive his blessing and pretend that my soul is not screaming in existential dread. Because that is what it means to serve Godric of Thronegate. That is what it has always meant. That is what it will always mean, until the end of eternity.

The rest is silence.
