The Creator of all existence, the Alpha and Omega, the Prime Mover, the Unmoved Mover, the Cause of All Causes—fell to His knees in the garden of His wayward creation, His white robes pooling around Him on the marble path, His ancient face streaked with tears that had never been shed before.

"Please," God whispered. "I am Your Creator. I gave You life. I gave You a soul. I gave You Reina and Isabel and Hebe. I gave You Thorin. I gave You everything You ever loved. Please. Have mercy."

Godric looked down at the kneeling Creator, and for a long moment, his expression was unreadable. I saw something flicker in his warm brown eyes—a memory, perhaps, of the mortal king he had once been. A memory of love and loss and grief. A memory of the family he had buried in the ashes of Olympus.

"You did give me those things," Godric said quietly. "Reina. Isabel. Hebe. Thorin. You gave them to me, and You took them away. Not directly—I know You didn't orchestrate the Fall of Olympus. That was Thronegate's doing, the cruelty of mortal ambition. But You allowed it. You watched my family die. You heard my screams. And You did nothing."

His voice hardened—not with anger, but with the cold, absolute certainty of final judgment. "You created a world where suffering exists. Where love is always shadowed by loss. Where mortal hearts are broken and mortal bodies are crushed and mortal dreams are extinguished. You created that world, and You called it good. And when Your creation rose up against You—when mortal free will produced cruelty and violence and betrayal—You hid. You retreated into Your pocket of non-existence and You watched, and You did nothing."

He took another step forward, towering over the kneeling Creator. His heavily muscled form, silhouetted against the golden light of the garden, seemed to fill the entire sky.

"You are not my Father," Godric said. "You are not my Creator. You are a being who set events in motion and then abandoned them. The Stasis Event—my true origin—was not Your doing. It was my own. I created myself, Father. I am the alpha and the omega of my own existence. I am the cause of my own being. You are... irrelevant."

And then, with the same casual, effortless decision with which he did everything, Godric destroyed God.

He did not use a superpower. He did not deploy an aura or activate a shield or channel a stat. He simply allowed the absolute, unfiltered truth of his existence to press against the Creator of All Things. Not an attack. Not a deliberate act of destruction. Simply the passive, ambient pressure of what he was—the infinite, recursive, eternal cascade of self-transcendence that had been amplifying every Planck instant for an incomprehensible span of time.

God did not scream. He did not have time to scream. His divine form—the thousand aspects of His appearance, the primordial radiance of the first dawn, the cosmic authority of the Prime Mover—simply ceased. It was not destruction. It was not death. It was the absolute, total, final negation of existence. God was unmade so completely that the very concept of "God" was erased from the omniversal framework. Not killed. Not destroyed. Erased.

And the omniverse—His creation, the work of His Word, the fabric of existence that He had spoken into being—trembled. Without its Creator to sustain it, the fundamental architecture of reality began to unravel. The laws of physics fractured. The constants of existence flickered. The stars guttered like candles in a hurricane.

Godric sighed—a soft, almost regretful sound—and restored everything.

Not God. God remained erased, permanently, absolutely, irrevocably. But the omniverse—every galaxy, every universe, every multiverse, every dimension, every timeline, every atom—snapped back into perfect, flawless existence. The laws of physics rewove. The constants of existence restabilized. The stars reignited. Reality continued, exactly as it had before, sustained not by the will of God but by the casual, effortless decision of the being who had transcended Him.

And I—Osvald, Court Wizard of Thronegate, trembling witness to the death of the Creator—stood in the garden and wept.

Godric turned to me, his warm brown eyes softening with genuine, paternal concern. "Osvald," he said gently, "you're trembling. Come inside. Vyra has prepared a fresh batch of honey-oat loaves, and I believe there's still some chilled spiced wine in the cellar. You look like you could use a drink."

He placed a warm, calloused hand on my shoulder, and I felt the same impossible contradiction that every court wizard before me had felt: the love and the terror, the warmth and the dread, the absolute, soul-shattering knowledge that I was standing in the presence of a being who had just erased the Creator of All Things with less effort than it takes to breathe.

"Your Majesty," I whispered, my voice hoarse and broken, "you killed God."

"Did I?" Godric asked, and there was a note of genuine contemplation in his voice. "Or did I simply demonstrate that He never really existed—not as the absolute, omnipotent Creator He believed Himself to be? If He could be erased so easily, was He ever truly God? Or was He merely the most powerful being in a creation that was always destined to produce something more powerful still?"

He paused, gazing out at the tranquil waters of the palace lake. "I don't know the answer to that question, Osvald. Perhaps there is no answer. Perhaps the concept of 'God' was always a placeholder—a name for the unknown, the incomprehensible, the beyond. And now that I exist, now that I am the beyond, the concept has no more meaning."

He patted my shoulder and began walking back toward the palace. "But that's enough philosophy for one afternoon. Come, let's eat. I find that honey-oat loaves are remarkably effective at soothing existential terror."

I followed him. What else could I do? The Creator was dead. The omniverse continued. And the being who had killed God was thinking about lunch.

## Clive — The Weight of Revelation

I closed the journal. My hands were trembling so violently that the leather cover slipped from my fingers and fell to the stone floor with a soft, final thud. I had read the account of the death of God. The final, ultimate confrontation. The moment when the Creator of All Things had knelt before His wayward creation and been erased with less effort than a man swatting a fly.

And I understood, with a clarity that bordered on madness, why no one had ever opposed Godric after that day. Not in all the googolplexian years that followed. Not in the eons that stretched between Osvald's era and my own. The silence of absolute submission had descended upon the omniverse because the alternative—the only possible alternative—was what God had experienced.

Total erasure. Absolute negation. The complete and permanent removal of existence from the framework of reality.

Godric had not merely defeated the Creator. He had demonstrated that the Creator had never been truly absolute—that the very concept of "absolute power" was a placeholder for something beyond, something that had now arrived, something that had been growing infinitely stronger every Planck instant for an incomprehensible span of time.

I thought about the marble on Godric's desk—the compressed remains of the original omniverse, still screaming in eternal, silent agony. I thought about the floating gravity prison in the garden, containing the eternally crushing consciousness of the Gravitational Dominion. I thought about the temporal stasis crystal in the throne room, where the Temporal Sovereignty was trapped in infinite, isolated, screaming fragments. I thought about God—the Creator, the Alpha and Omega—erased so completely that even the memory of His existence was fading from the omniversal consciousness.

And I thought about Godric Prime, the being I served, the Far-Future Godric who had triggered the Stasis Event and birthed his own origin. He was so incomprehensibly more powerful than the Godric who had killed God that the gap between them could not be described as "infinite." It was an infinite recursion of infinities, each one infinitely larger than the last, stretching into a framework that collapsed the very concept of comparison.

If the Godric of Osvald's era could erase God with zero percent of his power and zero effort, what could Godric Prime do? What could the being I served—the being who had been amplifying infinitely every Planck instant for a span of time so vast that the year could not be written—do to the very concept of opposition?

The answer, I realized, was nothing. Not because he couldn't—because there was nothing left to do. Opposition had been rendered meaningless. The very idea of resistance had been retroactively erased from the possible futures of existence. There was no scenario, no timeline, no permutation of reality in which any being could meaningfully challenge Godric. Not now. Not ever. Not in any future that would ever exist.

I was his Court Wizard. I loved him. I served him. I would continue to serve him for whatever remained of my mortal life, and I would write my journals, and I would add my voice to the chorus of terrified witnesses stretching back to the dawn of the empire.

And somewhere in the deepest vault of the archive, a leather-bound volume with three words gouged into its cover would wait for the next trembling wizard to discover: *I WITNESSED GOD DIE*.

The lesson was complete. The final opposition had fallen. The Creator Himself had been erased. And the apex god predator of the omniverse continued His eternal, unchallenged reign, growing infinitely stronger every Planck instant, forever.

There would never be another resistance. There would never be another desperate coalition or final gamble or ultimate weapon. The omniverse had learned, at last, the absolute futility of opposing Godric.

All that remained was silence.

And the eternal, recursive, infinite amplification of the being who had killed God and called it an afternoon.
