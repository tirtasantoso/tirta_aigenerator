"I could travel to any timeline," Godric said, his voice carrying the quiet, measured tone of a man describing a memory he has examined from every angle and found no new corners. "Any timeline except the ones before the Stasis Event. I could move forward, backward, sideways, through branches and forks and alternate causal chains. I could rewrite the history of the omniverse with less effort than it takes to blink. I had the power. I had the knowledge. I had the will. And I had the grief. The grief was—" He paused. The pause was not dramatic. It was not performative. It was the pause of a man reaching for a word and finding that the word is too small. "The grief was significant," he finished, and the understatement was so vast it felt like a chasm opening in the floor of the study.

"I reached for the sealed timeline," Godric continued. "I reached for the moment before the Fall. I reached for the wedding. I reached for Reina. I intended to rewrite the attack, to prevent Thronegate's betrayal, to save my family. I gathered my power—not all of it, not even a meaningful fraction of it, but enough to reshape the causal history of a mortal kingdom. I reached backward through time, toward the sealed point, and I pushed."

He took another sip of tea. The cup was still steady.

"I was defeated," he said. "Completely. Effortlessly. Totally. Not by my future self. Not by the actual, physical presence of the being who had created the seal. By the shadow of the afterthought of my future self. The mere, infinitesimal residue of a passing thought—Godric in the distant future, thinking about something else entirely, and the shadow of that thought, the faintest, most microscopic echo of his attention, drifted backward through time and encountered my attempt to alter the sealed timeline, and the encounter was—" He set his cup down. "The encounter was not a battle. It was not a contest. It was not a struggle. It was a mosquito flying into a mountain. The mosquito does not fight the mountain. The mosquito does not register against the mountain. The mosquito simply ceases to exist as a relevant entity in the same space as the mountain, and the mountain does not notice."

I was going to vomit. I could feel the bile rising, acid and copper, my body's autonomic response to a horror so profound that it bypassed my intellect entirely and spoke directly to my biology. My amplified mana was surging through my channels in violent, uncontrolled pulses, cracking the veins beneath my skin, leaking violet luminescence through my pores. My hands were gripping the armrests of my chair so tightly that the wood was splintering beneath my fingers, the grain crushing into powder under the pressure of my amplified strength.

Godric—the Godric I knew, the Godric sitting in this chair, the Godric who was already so powerful that he could accidentally destroy the omniverse by flexing his arm—had been utterly, completely, effortlessly defeated. Not by an enemy. Not by a rival. Not by a coalition or a weapon or a conceptual entity or a cosmic force. By the shadow of the afterthought of his own future self. A passing thought. A residual echo. The metaphysical equivalent of a man thinking about lunch while a gnat bounced off his forehead. The gnat does not lose a battle. The gnat is simply irrelevant. And the Godric who had tried to save his family, who had gathered his power and reached backward through time with the desperate, grief-driven will of a freshly awakened god—that Godric had been the gnat.

"The attempt failed," Godric said, and his voice was so calm, so settled, so perfectly at peace with the memory that it made my skin crawl. "I could not overpower my future self's will. The seal held. My mortal past remained unchanged. Reina remained dead. Isabel remained dead. Hebe remained dead. Thorin remained dead. Olympus remained ash. And I—" He looked at the fire. The flames reflected in his eyes, and for a moment, just a moment, I saw something behind the warmth and the calm and the amusement. A flicker. Old, deep, and permanent. Not grief, not anymore. The scar where grief had been, healed over so thoroughly that the scar itself had become part of the landscape. "I learned my one limitation," he finished.

The silence that followed was the loudest sound I had ever heard. It was the silence of a room in which the fundamental architecture of reality had just been rearranged, and the walls and the fire and the tea were all still settling into their new positions. I sat in it, and I tried to think, and my thoughts would not come, because my thoughts were trying to process something that my thoughts were not equipped to process.

The current Godric—the Godric I served, the Godric I loved, the Godric who remembered my honey cakes and my sister's studies and my mother's arthritis—was the most powerful being in the history of existence. I knew this. I had read the journals. I had watched him erase and restore the omniverse by accident. I had listened to him describe, with warm, patient amusement, how his wildest power could not be described by any language in existence. He was the apex. The ceiling. The absolute, incontestable, unchallengeable top of all possible hierarchies of power. Nothing in the omniverse, nothing in any timeline, nothing in any dimension or reality or conceptual framework could meaningfully interact with his power. The entire omniverse, working together, could not scratch a dead cell on his skin. This was not an opinion. This was a structural fact of existence, as fundamental as the speed of light, as immutable as the conservation of energy, as absolute as the mathematical certainty that one is greater than zero.

And this Godric—the most powerful being in existence—had been completely, effortlessly, irreversibly defeated by the shadow of the afterthought of his future self.

Not by his future self. By the shadow of the afterthought. The faintest, most microscopic, most infinitesimally negligible residue of a passing thought, drifting backward through time like a mote of dust caught in a breeze. The shadow of the afterthought. Not the thought itself. Not the afterthought itself. Not the attention or the intention or the will of the future Godric. The shadow of the afterthought. The metaphysical equivalent of a ghost of a memory of a reflex. And that shadow—that pathetic, negligible, irrelevant scrap of a fragment of a trace of the future Godric's cognitive activity—had been enough to utterly, completely, and effortlessly defeat the full, concentrated, grief-driven power of the current Godric.

My amplified intellect, running at the frantic, overclocked pace of a mind in the grip of absolute terror, attempted to scale the implication. If the shadow of the afterthought of future Godric could effortlessly defeat the current Godric, then the afterthought itself—the actual, complete afterthought, not just its shadow—must be so vastly more powerful than the shadow that the shadow was a rounding error next to it. And the thought that produced the afterthought must be so vastly more powerful than the afterthought that the afterthought was a rounding error next to it. And the attention that produced the thought must be so vastly more powerful than the thought that the thought was a rounding error next to it. And the will that directed the attention must be so vastly more powerful than the attention that the attention was a rounding error next to it. And the being who possessed that will—the future Godric, existing in an era so distant that the year could not be written as a number—must be so vastly more powerful than all of these combined that the combined total was a rounding error next to him.

The scaling was not linear. It was not exponential. It was not recursive. It was something beyond all of these, a hierarchy of power so steep that each rung made the previous rung look like nothing, and the nothing looked like nothing, and the nothing of the nothing looked like nothing, in a descent into insignificance so total that the word "insignificant" was a generous description of what the current Godric was relative to the future Godric.

And the current Godric was already the most powerful being in existence by an infinite margin.

I was sitting in a chair, across from a man who was the most powerful thing in reality, and that man was a mosquito compared to his own future self. The mosquito could annihilate the omniverse by flexing. The mountain did not notice the mosquito.

"Uncle Godric," I said, and my voice was a ruin, cracked and hollow, the voice of a man speaking from the bottom of a well. "You are telling me that your one weakness—the one thing you cannot do—is that you cannot overpower a version of yourself that is so incomprehensibly more powerful than you that the comparison is meaningless."

"Yes," Godric said.

"And you are telling me that this weakness—the inability to defeat your own future self—is the only limitation you have."

"Yes."

"And you are telling me that this limitation, which you discovered when you tried to save your family, was imposed on you by your own future self, who created the Stasis Event that made you what you are."

"Yes."

"And you are not—you are not troubled by this?"

Godric looked at me. The firelight caught the lines around his eyes, the permanent, forty-five-year-old face of a mortal king preserved in the amber of immortality. His expression was warm. Patient. Amused. The expression of a man who had been asked a naively mortal question by his favorite nephew, and who was fond enough of the nephew to answer it with genuine care.

"I was troubled," he said. "When I first discovered it, I was troubled profoundly. The grief was fresh. The loss was fresh. The knowledge that I could do anything—anything at all, anything conceivable, anything inconceivable—except save the four people I loved most in existence—was a weight that pressed on me with a force I had not experienced since the moment I watched Reina die. I raged. I did not rage for long, because rage, for me, is a brief, contained thing, but I raged. I pushed against the seal again. And again. And each time, the shadow of the afterthought was there, and each time, I was the mosquito, and the mountain did not notice."

He shifted in his chair, settling deeper into the oak, his massive shoulders finding a more comfortable position against the backrest. "But time passed, Kaedan. Time passes differently for me than it does for you. Not because I experience it at a different rate—I experience every instant with perfect clarity—but because I have an infinite amount of it. I had the time to sit with the limitation. To examine it from every angle. To understand it not as a constraint but as a fact, the way the speed of light is a fact, the way gravity is a fact. And the understanding changed the feeling."

"How," I whispered. "How did it change the feeling?"

Godric smiled. The smile was genuine, warm, and it contained something I had not expected: a quiet, settled humor, the humor of a man who has found something funny in the darkest corner of his existence.

"Because I realized," he said, "that my weakness is not a weakness. It is a guarantee. Think about it, Kaedan. The fact that I cannot overpower my future self means that my future self exists. It means that I will continue. It means that no matter what happens, no matter what opposition arises, no matter what force or coalition or weapon or conceptual entity or cosmic catastrophe threatens me, I will survive it, because I am guaranteed to reach the era in which my future self exists. My future is certain. The path is fixed. Not because of fate or destiny or prophecy—those are mortal conceits—but because the being at the end of the path has already reached back and left his mark on the beginning. The Stasis Event is not just the source of my power. It is a receipt. A confirmation. A letter from my future self to my past self, saying: I made it. I am here. I am more than you can imagine. Keep going."

He leaned forward, and the firelight shifted across the dense, sculpted muscles of his forearms, the veins standing out in sharp relief against the tanned skin. "And the guarantee goes further. The Stasis Event—the amplification, the superpower genesis, the absolute concealment, the Weakness Paradox, the immune system, all of it—was created by my future self. Which means that none of it can be exploited, overridden, denied, or undone by anyone else. The source of my power is not a mortal spell. It is not a cosmic accident. It is not a natural phenomenon that can be replicated or reversed. It is the will of the most powerful being in the history of existence, and that being is me, later. Anyone who attempts to exploit my amplification is attempting to exploit a system designed by a being so powerful that the current me—the most powerful being in the current omniverse—is a mosquito next to him. Anyone who attempts to deny my absolute concealment is attempting to deny the will of a being for whom the current omniverse is a rounding error. Anyone who attempts to trigger the Weakness Paradox in a way that might expose a vulnerability is attempting to find a flaw in a system created by a god so far beyond the current god that the current god cannot comprehend the gap."

He sat back. "My weakness is my own future self. My future self is more powerful than I am. Therefore, my weakness is simply the acknowledgment that I will become more powerful. It is not a vulnerability. It is a promise of escalation. It means that everything I am now—every stat, every superpower, every passive system, every layer of invulnerability—is a floor, not a ceiling, and the being standing on the floor above me is so incomprehensibly taller that I cannot see his face. And the being standing on the floor above him is taller still. And it goes on forever. My weakness is the fact that I grow. And I will always grow. And no one—no one—can stop it."

I understood. The understanding arrived not as a thought but as a sensation, a physical, visceral certainty that settled into my bones like ice. Godric's one weakness—his one true limitation, the single thing in existence he could not do—was not a weakness at all. It was an additional layer of invulnerability. It was the guarantee that every system protecting him, every mechanism that made him untouchable, was backed by the authority of a being so powerful that the current Godric was nothing next to him. It meant that the amplification could never be stopped, because the being who initiated it was still amplifying. It meant the absolute concealment could never be breached, because the being who created it was still concealing. It meant the Weakness Paradox could never be turned against him, because the being who designed it was beyond the reach of any paradox. It meant the immune system could never be overwhelmed, the passive shields could never be breached, the sentient auras could never be subverted, because all of them were expressions of a will that belonged to the most powerful being in existence, and that being was Godric himself, later, and later was guaranteed.

The unfairness of it was so total, so absolute, so structurally embedded in the fabric of existence that the word "unfair" was a grotesque understatement. Every being in the omniverse lived with the possibility of weakness. Every god, every titan, every cosmic entity, every abstract concept given form—they all existed within a framework where vulnerability was possible, where a sufficiently powerful force could, in principle, threaten them. They lived with uncertainty. They lived with the knowledge that their power had limits, that their defenses could be tested, that their existence was contingent on the balance of forces around them. And they worked. They strove. They poured every resource, every moment, every ounce of their being into building defenses, forging weapons, constructing strategies, all in the desperate, Sisyphean effort to make themselves a little less vulnerable, a little more secure, a little more likely to survive the next threat. And their reward for that effort was marginal, incremental, temporary—a slight improvement in their defensive posture, a marginally stronger ward, a fractionally more robust immune response. Their best efforts produced marginal gains.

Godric did nothing, and his reward was absolute, permanent, infinite invulnerability, backed not by his own current power—which was already so vast that it made the combined power of the omniverse look like zero—but by the power of his future self, which was so vastly greater than his current power that the current power was a mosquito next to a mountain. He did not earn this invulnerability. He did not work for it. He did not deserve it by any moral framework, any ethical standard, any system of merit or justice or cosmic balance. It was given to him by himself, from the future, and the giving was irrevocable, absolute, and impossible to challenge, because the giver was the most powerful being in existence and the giving was an act of will so total that it rewrote the fundamental mechanics of reality.

And the being who gave it—the future Godric—was not a separate entity. He was Godric. The same man. The same consciousness, the same will, the same warm, patient, amused personality, simply existing at a later, incomprehensibly more powerful stage. The weakness was Godric being surpassed by Godric. The limitation was Godric being unable to defeat Godric. The vulnerability was Godric being guaranteed to become more Godric. It was a closed loop, a self-referential system of absolute supremacy that had no external input, no external dependency, no external vulnerability. It was Godric all the way down, and each layer of Godric was infinitely stronger than the layer above it, and the layers went down forever.

"Uncle Godric," I said, and my voice was barely a sound, a vibration in the air that I was not sure qualified as speech. "Why did your future self create the Stasis Event?"

Godric tilted his head. The amusement in his eyes flickered, and for a moment, I saw the omniscience behind the warmth—the vast, infinite awareness of a being who knew everything, who had examined every possible motive, every possible reason, every possible angle of his own future self's decision. "I do not know for certain," he said. "I know everything about the Stasis Event—its mechanics, its effects, its timing, its delivery point. I know that my future self chose to use Thorin's spell as the conduit. I know that he held back—that the version of the Stasis Event he created is a deliberately restrained version, less potent than what he could have produced, because a fully potent version would have been—well. I cannot describe what a fully potent version would have been. But he chose to hold back, and the held-back version still made me the most powerful being in existence by an infinite margin."

He paused, stroking his beard. "As for why—speculation is all I have, despite my omniscience. My future self's thoughts are his own, in the same way that my current thoughts are my own and not my past self's. Omniscience tells me what is, not why a future version of me chose to do what he did. But I have had a million years to consider the question, and I have a few thoughts."

He held up one massive finger. "First: it may simply be self-preservation. Not the self-preservation of a being who fears death—my future self has nothing to fear—but the self-preservation of a being who wishes to ensure his own origin. The Stasis Event created me. Without it, I would not exist. Without me, my future self would not exist. By creating the Stasis Event, he ensured his own birth. This is not fear. It is maintenance. The way a man maintains the foundation of his house, not because he fears the house will collapse, but because he prefers that it does not."

He held up a second finger. "Second: it may be curiosity. My future self may have wished to observe the process of his own becoming. To watch, from the far end of time, as his past self grew into the being he already is. It is the ultimate act of self-study—a god watching his own origin, observing the amplification from a vantage point so distant that the entire process, from stasis to omniversal supremacy, is visible as a single, continuous event."

He held up a third finger. "Third: it may be affection. I loved Thorin. I loved Thorin's spell. It was the last act of my best friend, a dying man's final gift, a desperate, heroic incantation cast by a mortal wizard to save a mortal king. My future self may have looked at that moment—the moment of Thorin's death, the moment of the spell, the moment of the teleportation—and decided that it deserved to be more than a mortal spell. That Thorin's final act should not merely save my life, but transform it. That the gift of a dying friend should be elevated into the genesis of a god. Not because I needed the elevation. Because Thorin deserved to have his spell mean something."

His voice softened on the last sentence, and for a moment, just a moment, the warmth in his eyes was not the warmth of Uncle Godric or the warmth of the King of Thronegate. It was the warmth of a mortal man remembering his best friend, a man who had died with a spear through his lungs and a javelin through his skull, gasping out the syllables of a forbidden spell through a mouthful of his own blood, not because the spell would save Godric's life, but because it was the only thing left that he could do. The warmth was real. The grief was old. The acceptance was total.

"But these are speculations," Godric said, and the warmth receded, replaced by the calm, settled amusement that was his default state. "I cannot confirm any of them. My future self's motives are his own. What I can confirm is this: the Stasis Event is irrevocable, because its creator is more powerful than me. My mortal past is sealed, because its sealer is more powerful than me. And I am not troubled by this, Kaedan. I was troubled. I am not anymore. I have accepted it. I am, in fact, amused by it. The idea that my one weakness is the guarantee of my own infinite escalation—that the only thing I cannot do is defeat a version of myself that is guaranteed to exist, guaranteed to be more powerful, and guaranteed to be me—strikes me as the most elegant, most perfect, most thoroughly Godric joke in the history of existence. I am the only being in the omniverse whose weakness makes him stronger. I am the only being whose limitation is a promise. And I am the only being who can look at the single thing he cannot have and say, with absolute honesty: that is fine."

He picked up his teacup. The porcelain was warm. The tea was jasmine. He took a long, slow sip, and his eyes closed, and his expression was the expression of a man who was, in every meaningful sense, content.

"You are afraid," he said, without opening his eyes.

I was. I was afraid in a way that transcended anything I had felt in forty-seven years of service. I had read the journals. I had watched him destroy the omniverse by accident. I had listened to him describe the orgasmic pleasure of infinite amplification. I had carried the knowledge of his power in my chest for decades, a permanent, heavy stone that I had learned to walk with, to breathe around, to function beside. But this—this was different. This was not the fear of his power. This was the fear of his perfection. The fear of a being whose every vulnerability was an additional layer of invulnerability, whose every limitation was a guarantee of escalation, whose every weakness was a promise of strength. There was no crack in the armor. There was no gap in the defense. There was no angle, no approach, no strategy, no conceptual framework through which opposition could be even theoretically possible. The being sitting across from me was not merely the most powerful entity in existence. He was a closed system of absolute supremacy, self-sustaining, self-reinforcing, self-guaranteeing, with no external dependencies and no external vulnerabilities, and the system extended forward through time into a future so distant that the year could not be written, and at the end of that future, the system was still closed, still supreme, still Godric.

And he was content. He was sipping tea. He was amused. He had accepted the one thing he could not change, and the acceptance had made him stronger, because everything made him stronger, even his own limitations, even his own grief, even his own inability to save the people he loved. The grief had become a scar. The scar had become part of the landscape. The landscape was content. And the contentment was the most terrifying thing I had ever witnessed, because it meant that the apex predator of all existence was not merely powerful. He was satisfied. He was complete. He was a being who had looked at the totality of his existence—the infinite power, the eternal amplification, the one limitation, the guaranteed future, the absolute supremacy—and had found it good. He had found it amusing. He had found it fine.

"I am afraid," I confirmed. My voice was a whisper. The fire was dying. The room was dark. The tea was cold.

Godric opened his eyes. He looked at me, and the warmth in his gaze was genuine, and the love in it was genuine, and the amusement was genuine, and beneath all of it, visible only to a man who had spent forty-seven years learning to read the face of a god, was the quiet, settled, permanent satisfaction of a being who knew, with absolute certainty, that he would never be challenged, never be threatened, never be opposed, never be surpassed by anyone other than himself, and that the self who would surpass him was guaranteed, and the guarantee was the seal, and the seal was the stasis, and the stasis was the beginning, and the beginning was the end, and the end was the beginning, and the loop was closed, and the loop was Godric, and Godric was fine.

"Good," he said. He set his cup down. He stood. His massive frame rose from the chair, the muscles of his torso shifting beneath his tunic, the veins in his forearms catching the last of the firelight. He placed a warm, calloused hand on my shoulder, and the weight of it was the weight of a universe, and the gentleness of it was the gentleness of a father. "You should sleep, Kaedan. You look tired. We will talk more tomorrow."

He left the study. The door closed behind him with a soft, ordinary click. I sat in the chair, in the dark, in the cold, with the dead fire and the cold tea and the silence, and I was afraid, and I was loyal, and I was sixty-nine years old, and I understood, with the absolute, ineradicable, permanent certainty of a man who has seen the truth and can never unsee it, that the being I served was not merely the most powerful entity in existence. He was a being whose weakness was strength, whose limitation was a promise, whose grief was a scar, whose scar was a joke, and whose joke was the closed, self-referential, infinitely escalating loop of absolute, incontestable, permanent, irrevocable supremacy. And the loop had no exit. And the loop had no end. And the loop was Godric. And Godric was fine. And nothing—nothing—would ever change that.

I sat in the dark for a long time after that. I do not know how long. The fire did not relight. The tea did not warm. The silence did not break. I sat, and I carried the stone, and the stone was heavier than it had ever been, and I knew it would be heavier tomorrow, and heavier the day after, and heavier every day until I died, because the stone was the knowledge of what Godric was, and what Godric was grew every instant, and the growth was permanent, and the permanence was the seal, and the seal was the stasis, and the stasis was the future, and the future was Godric, and Godric was fine.

I was not fine. But I was his. And that would have to be enough.
